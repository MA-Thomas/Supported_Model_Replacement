COMMUNICATION JOB
By the end, technically trained lab members who know AUROC should be able to
explain why AP needs a declared prevalence, why a robust claim must survive a
prevalence set and an evaluation-generating regime, and how paired tie-neutral
support can justify—or decline to justify—model replacement.

VISUAL SYSTEM
16:9, warm off-white field, navy text, blue for population/prevalence,
orange for tension/failure, teal for resolutions/supported claims, and plum
for replication regimes. Titles are complete pedagogical claims or questions.
Small examples recur and accumulate. Visible text remains sparse; technical
qualification and source blocks live in speaker notes.

MAIN DECK
01  What did the performance claim survive?
02  AUROC describes separation—but our decisions receive a selected set
03  Precision and recall ask complementary questions about that selected set
04  A PR curve records what happens as the selected set grows
05  Average precision rewards positive cases that appear early in the ranking
06  The ranking can stay unchanged while precision collapses
07  The criterion is to change the prior without changing within-class ranking
08  Prior-standardized precision makes the target prevalence explicit
09  Reweighting is licensed only when class-conditional scores transport
10  AP needs a common baseline before prevalences can be compared
11  Chance normalization aligns the endpoints—not the whole profile
12  If the claim must hold everywhere, an average answers the wrong question
13  A performance claim needs three declarations: populations, regime, attempts
14  “Replication” is incomplete until we say what is allowed to change
15  Prevalence and regime belong to one assessment—but enter in different ways
16  Expected performance can conceal a replication that defeated the claim
17  Support is the expected level retained by every declared challenge
18  At K = 2, corroboration charges exactly half the replication disagreement
19  In Popper’s sense, survival is support—not probability of truth
20  A bootstrap estimates projected support; it does not manufacture replications
21  Population challenge must occur inside each complete replication
22  The full construction separates mean performance from two costs of challenge
23  A fixed-model bootstrap preserves the order of the scientific claim
24  Finite-sample AP has a design anchor that need not equal prevalence
25  Conditional calibration makes one model interpretable—but not comparable
26  Subtracting two marginal reports combines adverse cases that never co-occurred
27  Ordinary pairing can still reward a model for arbitrary tie-breaking
28  Tie averaging removes expected credit for distinctions the model did not make
29  The paired advantage must be formed before either challenge is applied
30  Tie neutrality needs an exchangeable-label reference law
31  Coherent replacement permits a no-verdict region
32  Replacement requires evidence above a practical margin—not merely a positive estimate
33  The number is meaningful only together with the assessment it survived
34  The framework turns “performance” into a criticizable scientific claim
